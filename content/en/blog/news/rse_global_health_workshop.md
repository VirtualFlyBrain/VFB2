On 20 October 2026 the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://rse.org.uk) (RSE), Scotland's National Academy, held its Global Health Workshop at its George Street home in Edinburgh. The day brought together researchers, clinicians and policy makers from across Scotland with colleagues from Malawi, Kenya, South Africa, India, Pakistan, Malaysia and Taiwan. Six sessions covered artificial intelligence in health systems, nutrition and climate, water, mental health and health inequalities, with a student poster session over lunch.

Virtual Fly Brain (VFB) took part in the third session, 'Small Brains, Big Threats: Connectomics and the Insects Shaping Global Health', chaired by David Price (University of Edinburgh). Douglas Armstrong, VFB's Principal Investigator at Edinburgh, spoke alongside Liz Bandason (Lilongwe University of Agriculture and Natural Resources), Andrea Adden (University of Bristol) and Kit Longden (University of Cambridge).

Most of the room worked outside fly neuroscience, so the talk set out what a connectome is and why a fly brain is worth mapping before it turned to VFB itself. Rather than slides of text, we built the talk as a series of short movies made from VFB data: neurons that differ between male and female brains, shown in 3D (the movie above), and how VFB links a neuron traced in an electron microscopy connectome to the driver lines a lab can order to study it.

The worked example was carbon dioxide. Flies detect it with receptor neurons on the antenna, and the connectomes on VFB let you follow that signal into the brain, connection by connection. Carbon dioxide is also one of the cues a mosquito uses to find a person, which is where a fly circuit meets the session's theme.

We also showed how people who are not fly anatomists can now ask VFB questions in plain language, through [VFB chat](https://chat.virtualflybrain.org) and through AI assistants connected to our [MCP tool](/docs/tutorials/vfb-mcp-guide/). The answers come from the curated database, with links back to the terms, images and publications behind them. All of the data are open, and VFB is already used by researchers around the world. Our plans for the next phase of the project came down to three things: new users, new questions and new use cases.

Robert Court (Lead DevOps) attended with Douglas. Our thanks go to the RSE for the invitation and for a day that put insect brains on the same programme as health systems and policy.

If you would like to try what we showed, the [VFB Self-led Learning Sessions](https://workshop.virtualflybrain.org) are free and open to anyone, and need little or no setup.
